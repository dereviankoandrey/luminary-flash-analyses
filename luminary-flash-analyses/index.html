🏭 Dallas-Fort Worth Part One

The Infrastructure Convergence Play — Where Three Megatrends Collide in the World's Most Undervalued Major Metro

🔍 Macro Thesis

Dallas-Fort Worth is experiencing a rare convergence of three independent infrastructure migration waves that are mutually reinforcing, not substitutable:

1. Toyota Motor North America's Plano-to-DFW expansion (announced 2024): The Tokyo Stock Exchange-listed automotive giant is moving 5,000+ corporate jobs from its long-standing Illinois headquarters to a new DFW campus in Plano/Frisco. This isn't speculative — ground was broken Q1 2025, with Phase 1 occupancy targeted for late 2026. Toyota's employee housing packages historically include significant rental subsidies.

2. Amazon HQ2 in Arlington: The $2.5B second headquarters commitment (announced 2019, construction ongoing) is entering its final build phase with ~10,000 jobs planned for occupancy by 2027. Amazon's DFW presence already includes 40+ fulfillment centers; HQ2 transforms them from logistics nodes to a corporate employment anchor.

3. Google + Tesla Giga Texas data center demand: North Texas has become the nation's second-largest data center market after Northern Virginia, driven by cheap power (TXERC grid independence), fiber infrastructure, and no state income tax. The resulting construction/IT employment spillover creates parallel rental demand in suburbs not traditionally associated with tech migration.

The structural insight: Unlike Phoenix (film workers = renters only) or Atlanta (corporate HQs = mixed buyers/renters), DFW's convergence means both rental demand AND homebuyer demand are expanding simultaneously across different price tiers — a rare dual-engine market. Toyota targets middle-income engineers (rental demand, $2-3K/mo budget), Amazon targets senior corporate staff (homebuyers, $500K-$1M range), and data center employment spans both segments.

Toyota's historical employee housing program typically subsidizes $300-500/mo per household toward rental costs near corporate campuses, effectively creating a guaranteed demand floor.

⚠️ Risk Flags

⛔Toyota Timeline Uncertainty: Toyota's DFW campus construction has faced typical Texas permitting delays. Phase 1 was originally targeted for Q4 2025 — if delayed to mid-2026, the rental demand floor in Plano/Frisco shifts later than projected. Monitor Texas Department of Licensing and Regulation (TDLR) building permits for campus site.
⛔Data Center Employment Multiplier Overestimation: Data centers are highly automated — each facility creates ~20-50 direct jobs, not the hundreds often cited in press releases. The rental demand effect is primarily through indirect spillover (construction workers, support services) rather than direct data center employment. This doesn't invalidate the thesis but reduces the magnitude of the data-center-specific component.
⛔Texas Insurance Crisis: Homeowners insurance in Texas has increased 25-40% since 2023 due to hail/wind risk and carrier withdrawals from the state market. This directly impacts cash flow on rental properties (insurance is a hard cost that cannot be deferred). Factor +15-25% into pro forma carrying costs vs national averages.
⛔New Construction Oversupply Risk: DFW has the highest new single-family construction volume of any US metro (~40K units/year). New builds compete directly with flips on features/quality, compressing ARV margins. The flip thesis works best for pre-2005 properties in established neighborhoods where new construction doesn't exist at comparable prices.
